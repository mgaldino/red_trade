## Overall Feedback

Here are some overall reactions to the document.

**Outline**

The paper has a promising idea: discrete trade-rank changes may matter politically even when trade volumes move continuously. The current design, however, does not yet separate the proposed status mechanism from China-demand shocks, Brazilian foreign-policy change, and issue-specific UNGA dynamics strongly enough for the causal claims made in the abstract and conclusion.

The contribution is interesting because it shifts the trade-and-alignment literature from smooth dependence to public rank thresholds. The Brazil case is well chosen and the paper has useful triangulation across SDiD, issue-area voting, media salience, and cross-country evidence. The main weakness is that the strongest empirical pieces still do not identify the status cue as distinct from correlated economic and diplomatic changes around 2009.

**The rank-threshold effect is not cleanly separated from the China demand shock**

Section 3.1 and Table 3 frame the 2003, 2005, and 2012 timing tests as evidence against a continuous-growth explanation, but those tests do not fully isolate the political meaning of the rank threshold. The 2009 treatment bundles China becoming number one with the global financial crisis, the commodity cycle, Brazil's growing China export concentration, and a wider BRICS/G20 diplomatic moment. Earlier pseudo-years with smaller China shares do not answer whether a sharp commodity-demand shock in 2008-2009, rather than the public rank cue, explains the outcome movement. This matters because the paper's contribution depends on showing that categorical status does work beyond trade dependence and material exposure. A stronger revision should add designs that compare the rank threshold to smooth export shares, export concentration, commodity-price exposure, and the rank margin in the same framework, and should include placebo thresholds such as China becoming number two or non-China partners becoming number one. The paper should also report whether the Brazil estimate survives controls or matching for commodity exporters exposed to the same China demand shock.

**The SDiD design needs fuller diagnostics for a one-treated-unit claim**

Figure 2 says the pre-treatment fit is close enough, and Table 2 reports several specifications, but the paper does not give enough information to judge the synthetic comparison. Appendix Figure 7 lists only the top donor weights, while the main text does not report pre-treatment RMSPE, covariate balance, time weights, the full placebo distribution, or sensitivity to removing high-weight donors. With Brazil as the only treated unit, these diagnostics are not secondary; they are the basis for believing the counterfactual. The donor pool also raises a substantive concern because many controls were exposed to China's trade expansion, so stable-unit assumptions may be strained. A revision should report the complete SDiD weight structure, pre-treatment balance tables, RMSPE ratios, placebo ranks, jackknife estimates over major donor countries, and window sensitivity around 1997-2015. It should also explain why the global donor pool is preferable substantively, not only because the Latin America pool has larger standard errors.

**UNGA distance does not yet show selective alignment toward China rather than broader repositioning**

The main outcome in Section 3 is absolute ideal-point distance to China, and Figure 6 argues that China is stable while Brazil moves. That helps, but it does not establish that Brazil adjusted toward China because of the rank cue rather than moving along a broader Lula-era or post-2008 foreign-policy trajectory. Table 4 then uses simple pre/post identical vote shares by issue area, but those diagnostics are not estimated against the synthetic counterfactual and do not isolate votes where China and the United States diverged. The human-rights increase is important, yet the larger increase in the residual "Other / uncoded" category and the decline in economic development show that the issue pattern needs tighter interpretation. The paper should add a vote-level analysis for human-rights resolutions, ideally with resolution or agenda controls, showing whether Brazil switched from positions farther from China to positions closer to China after 2009 relative to comparable countries. It should also report parallel outcomes for distance to the United States and to other reference points, so readers can see whether the effect is China-specific or a more general movement away from Washington.

**The mechanism evidence shows salience, but not the status cue doing the causal work**

Section 5 provides useful media and official-discourse evidence, but it currently shows that China and China-Brazil trade became more visible after 2009. That is weaker than showing that the rank label itself changed what officials could justify. Figure 4 appears to count trade-related headlines, but the paper does not show the share of all China coverage, the total newspaper volume, a pre-trend break test, or the frequency of explicit labels such as "largest," "number one," or "principal partner." The official statements cited after Figure 4 also support diplomatic prioritization, but they are mostly about trade, investment, and bilateral institutions, not the UNGA voting choices where the treatment effect is measured. A revision should separate generic China-trade salience from explicit rank-status language, normalize headline counts, and show whether the 2009 break exceeds prior trends. The mechanism section would be much stronger with process evidence tying the rank cue to human-rights or UN voting decisions, for example Itamaraty statements, diplomatic cables, speeches around specific votes, or contemporaneous debates about Brazil's human-rights positions.

**The treatment language and the public cue are not fully aligned**

Section 3 correctly defines treatment as China becoming Brazil's largest export destination, but the introduction, media discussion, and official quotations often use broader "top trade partner" or "principal trading partner" language. This is more than wording. If the theory is about public labels, then the label used by media and officials may be total trade-partner status rather than export-destination rank. The paper says "top trade partner" is shorthand, but the mechanism depends on exactly what the public shorthand was. A revision should document whether the 2009 public discourse referred to exports, total trade, imports plus exports, or a general commercial hierarchy, and then align the treatment definition with that evidence. If the public cue was broader than export rank, the paper should either redefine the treatment accordingly or explain why export-destination rank is the correct causal threshold despite the broader language in the discourse.

**The cross-country design conditions on future treatment persistence**

Section 6 defines treated countries as those where China becomes the largest export destination and then remains there through the end of the panel. That creates a post-entry selection problem because countries are classified using future export-rank stability, which may be related to the same economic and political processes that affect UNGA alignment. Table 7 shows that short-lived entries have smaller estimates, but treating persistence as the main treatment after observing the full path risks selecting cases where China ties were already unusually stable, salient, or institutionally embedded. This matters because the paper uses the cross-country result to support a causal interpretation beyond Brazil. A cleaner design would define treatment using information available at entry, then model persistence, exits, and short-lived entries as treatment heterogeneity or dynamic exposure. At minimum, the paper should re-estimate the panel with all first entries, include event-time indicators for losing top status, and present the persistent-treatment sample as a scope condition rather than the main causal estimand.

**Cross-country inference is fragile with fourteen treated units**

Table 5 reports statistically significant IFE and C&S estimates, but the treated sample is only 14 countries and Table 8 shows that excluding Solomon Islands moves the main IFE estimate to p = 0.064. Table 9 also reports pretrend diagnostics with q = 12 and df2 equal to 2 or 1, which gives little basis for strong claims about pre-treatment fit. The text says the cross-country evidence makes a causal interpretation credible, but the evidence is better read as suggestive external-validity support. The small treated sample, heterogeneous cases, staggered timing, and sensitivity to one country all call for more restrained claims. A revision should report full leave-one-out results, randomization or permutation inference over treatment years, event-time support by period, simultaneous confidence bands, and estimates by displaced-incumbent type. The conclusion should describe the cross-country panel as a convergent check unless these additional diagnostics show the result is not driven by a few cases.

**No negative case for the status-cue mechanism**

The paper shows a high-salience positive case and a same-direction cross-country pattern, but it does not show a setting where the theory expects little or no movement. That leaves the status-cue mechanism too easy to satisfy: any durable China-top transition followed by convergence can be read as supportive, while the paper has no clear failed-threshold benchmark. This matters because the contribution depends on the claim that public recategorization, not just trade rank mechanically, does the political work. A useful addition would be a high- versus low-salience comparison among China-top entries: code whether local newspapers or official statements in the first two post-entry years used explicit labels such as “largest export destination,” “principal partner,” or “number one,” then estimate the same event-study pattern separately for public-uptake and no-public-uptake cases. If low-uptake cases show weak or no convergence, the status argument would have much sharper bite.

**Costly convergence is asserted rather than measured**

The human-rights section says this is the most informative area because movement toward China carried reputational costs for Brazil, but the paper does not operationalize those costs. Readers need to see that the post-2009 change is concentrated where the mechanism predicts adjustment should be hardest to justify. The current issue-area split is suggestive, yet “human rights” contains votes with very different stakes. The paper should add a resolution-level costliness measure, for example pre-2009 Brazil-China disagreement, whether the vote was country-specific, whether the United States/EU and China were on opposite sides, and Brazil’s own prior voting consistency. Then estimate whether Brazil’s post-2009 switches toward China are larger on high-cost human-rights resolutions than on lower-cost thematic votes.

**Continuous-interdependence baseline is not recovered formally**

The paper argues against smooth trade-dependence accounts, but it never formally recovers the standard continuous-exposure model as a benchmark. Controls inside SDiD and IFE specifications help identification, but they do not show how much the rank-threshold specification adds relative to the models used in the China-alignment literature. This is a missing comparison, not just a confounding concern. The paper should replicate a standard UNGA-alignment specification in the style of Flores-Macias and Kreps or Urdinez et al. on the same sample, with China trade share, US trade share, ideology, GDP, and regime covariates, and then add the rank-one entry indicator. Reporting changes in fit, event-time patterns, and the rank coefficient after continuous exposure is included would make the contribution easier to evaluate.

**Treatment coding rules are not fully portable**

The concept is meant to travel beyond Brazil, but the paper does not give enough implementation detail for another researcher to code trade-based status transitions consistently. The cross-country appendix lists absorbing treated cases, yet it omits the denominator: temporary entries, ties, missing-data cases, re-export hubs, previous incumbents, and cases excluded by the persistence rule. This matters because categorical status depends on coding thresholds, and small changes in persistence rules can change the treated group. Add a treatment-coding table for all China rank-one entries with first entry year, previous incumbent, export-share margin, duration at number one, exit year if any, and inclusion status under 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year persistence rules. That table would turn the status concept into a reusable measurement strategy rather than a case-specific classification.

**Recommendation**: major revision. The paper has a publishable core idea and a plausible high-leverage case, but the current evidence does not yet warrant the causal language used in the abstract, Section 6, and the conclusion. The revision needs to make the Brazil design more transparent, separate rank-status salience from correlated economic shocks, and downgrade or strengthen the cross-country claims.

**Key revision targets**:

1. Rework the Brazil identification section to separate the rank cue from China export share, commodity exposure, export concentration, the 2008-2009 crisis, and Lula-era foreign-policy change.
2. Add full SDiD diagnostics: pre-treatment RMSPE, covariate balance, full donor and time weights, placebo distribution, jackknife by donor, and window sensitivity.
3. Estimate issue-level and vote-level outcomes against an explicit counterfactual, especially for human-rights votes where China and the United States diverge.
4. Strengthen the mechanism evidence by measuring explicit rank-status language in media and official discourse, not only China-trade salience.
5. Redefine or reframe the cross-country treatment so persistence is not conditioned on future outcomes, and present small-N panel estimates with fuller sensitivity and more cautious language.

### 2. SDiD Equation Uses A Scalar Unit Weight

**Status**: [Pending]

**Quote**:
> The basic SDiD estimating equation is:
> 
> $$
> \left(\hat {\tau} _ {A T T} ^ {\mathrm {S D i D}}, \hat {\mu}, \hat {\alpha}, \hat {\beta}\right) = \underset {\tau_ {A T T}, \mu , \beta , \alpha} {\arg \min} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \left(Y _ {i t} - \mu - \alpha_ {i} - \beta_ {t} - D _ {i t} \tau_ {A T T}\right) ^ {2} \hat {w} ^ {\mathrm {S D i D}} \hat {\lambda} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm {S D i D}}

**Feedback**:
The time weight is indexed by t, but the unit weight is not indexed by i. As written, \hat w^{SDiD}=c>0 is a common multiplier, so it drops out of the argmin and cannot alter the relative influence of donor units. The display should use \hat w_i^{SDiD}\hat\lambda_t^{SDiD} inside the double sum. Without that index, the equation does not represent the unit-weighting step described in the text.

### 18. SCM Description Misstates Effect Timing And Invariant Heterogeneity

**Status**: [Pending]

**Quote**:
> ations show there is no unit fixed effect and no time weight in SCM. As a result, SCM cannot
> 
> <!-- PAGE BREAK -->
> 
> use unit-specific unobserved invariant characteristics to approximate the counterfactual outcome, and must consider the entire period when estimating the causal effect. SDiD combi

**Feedback**:
Two claims need correction. First, standard SCM fits donor weights using pre-treatment information and estimates effects from post-treatment treated-versus-synthetic gaps; it does not estimate the causal effect over the entire period. Second, omitting additive unit fixed effects does not mean SCM cannot approximate stable unit differences. For example, if Y_1t(0)=10+b_t, Y_2t(0)=b_t, and Y_3t(0)=20+b_t, weights w_2=w_3=1/2 exactly match the treated unit's invariant component. The safer distinction is that SCM handles stable differences through donor weights when the treated unit lies in the donor convex hull, not through additive fixed effects.

### 22. Latin America Donor Discussion Overstates Similarity

**Status**: [Pending]

**Quote**:
> When comparing results, the estimated causal effect is quite similar (a bit larger in absolute terms), which lends credibility to the main estimation. However, the small number of controls introduces noise and increases the uncertainty of the model. **Feedback**:
The point estimate is not only 'a bit larger.' The main estimate is about -0.26, while the Latin America donor-pool estimate is -0.41; the difference is 0.15, or roughly 58 percent of the main estimate's magnitude. The estimates may be statistically compatible, but the text should say the regional estimate has the same sign, is less precise, and is substantially larger in magnitude. Also, larger placebo-based uncertainty is a feature of the smaller sensitivity check, not a design reason by itself to prefer the global pool. Ground the donor-pool choice in counterfactual validity, not lower uncertainty.
