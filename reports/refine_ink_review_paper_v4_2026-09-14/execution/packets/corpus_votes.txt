969: However, restricting attention to a single issue domain opens a design that ideal points computed over all votes cannot support: a triple difference. Ideal points compress every resolution in a session into one score, leaving no contrast between issue domains to exploit, whereas vote-level data preserve it. Thus, I can not only compare the difference between Brazil and other untreated countries before and after (traditional DiD), but also include a third difference: across domains within the same country and year, comparing Brazil with the donor pool, before and after 2009, in human-rights votes relative to every other vote. Identification here asks for more than parallel trends between treated and control units: whatever bias a non-parallel trend introduces must be the same in the two issue domains, so that it cancels in the third difference [@olden_moen2022]. Appendix Figure \@ref(fig:plot-ddd-pretrends) displays the pre- and post-2009 Brazil-donor gaps in both domains; this descriptive comparison does not establish the identifying restriction.

972: \[
973: Y_{ir}=\alpha_i+\lambda_r+\beta(B_iP_r)+\gamma(B_iH_r)+\delta(B_iP_rH_r)+\varepsilon_{ir}.
974: \]
975: Country and resolution fixed effects absorb the remaining lower-order terms. The Brazil-by-domain interaction allows Brazil's pre-existing gap relative to donors to differ between human-rights and other resolutions. The coefficient $\delta$ measures the additional post-2009 human-rights shift. The sample contains `r format(ddd_hr_increment$n_obs, big.mark = ",")` observed votes by `r ddd_hr_increment$n_countries` countries on `r ddd_hr_increment$n_resolutions` China-US divergent resolutions in 2005--2012. Any human-rights tag places a resolution in the human-rights group; all remaining resolutions form the non-human-rights group. Each observed country-resolution vote receives equal weight. The specification does not absorb a separate domain intercept for each donor country; interpretation also requires changes in donor vote availability not to generate the differential shift across domains.

1055: # Brazilian Media Salience
1056: 
1057: To assess whether trade-topic salience increased around the rank reversal, I examine coverage of China in Folha de S.Paulo, Brazil's widest-circulation newspaper [@folha2016], from 2000 to 2014. Salience matters because the rank-threshold argument requires the top-rank cue to become politically usable. A change in export hierarchy can shape elite and public attention when it appears in a prominent national newspaper as a simple signal of China's commercial importance, consistent with work on media, agenda setting, and foreign policy [@soroka2003; @baum_potter2008; @walgraveContingencyMassMedias2006].
1058: 
1059: Headlines are the appropriate unit for this trade-topic diagnostic because they are tractable in large archives and operate as attention-directing cues [@zhang_yu2024_chinese_dream]. The relevant question is whether Folha increasingly foregrounded China as a trade relationship after the rank reversal. Headlines are the most visible textual cue in the archive and the part of coverage most likely to be scanned by broad audiences. Full article texts would be useful for a different exercise -- for example, measuring tone or detailed argumentation -- but the salience claim here is about which China-related themes were made visible at the headline level. This diagnostic does not measure the frequency of explicit rank labels.
