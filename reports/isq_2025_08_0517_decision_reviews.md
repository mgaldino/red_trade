## Reviewer 1

### Comments to the Author

This paper addresses an important and understudied question about how symbolic trade milestones affect foreign policy alignment. The core empirical design using Synthetic Difference-in-Differences (SDiD) to identify the causal effect of China becoming Brazil's top trade partner is methodologically sound and represents a valuable contribution. However, the paper requires major revisions to address significant theoretical and empirical gaps before it can be considered for publication.

### Major Issues Requiring Revision

#### 1. Fundamental Theoretical Gap: Media Salience -> Foreign Policy Mechanism

Core Problem: The paper's central claim is that media salience drives foreign policy changes, but it fails to establish a convincing causal pathway between increased newspaper coverage and UNGA voting convergence.

The authors state: "media outlets are interested in symbolic milestones when choosing their headlines. Should a salient one-off rank reversal, rather than gradual accumulation of trade, have lasting foreign-policy effects?" However, they never adequately explain HOW media salience would translate into diplomatic alignment.

Alternative explanations not adequately addressed:

- Economic dependence mechanism: Greater trade dependence simply reduces Brazil's margin to dissent from China regardless of media coverage.
- Elite interest alignment: Business lobbies may pressure for diplomatic alignment independent of media attention.
- Structural power effects: China's growing economic importance creates diplomatic pressure through material channels, not symbolic ones.

Required revision: The authors must develop a more sophisticated theoretical framework explaining the specific mechanism by which media salience influences foreign policy decision-making. This could include:

- How media coverage affects elite perceptions and decision-making processes.
- Whether public opinion mediates the relationship between media coverage and foreign policy.
- How media salience interacts with other mechanisms (economic dependence, lobbying, etc.).

#### 2. Weak Empirical Evidence for the Proposed Mechanism

Problem: The media analysis does not demonstrate that increased coverage actually influenced foreign policy decisions. The authors show correlation between media coverage and trade relationship changes, but fail to establish that media attention was the causal driver of diplomatic alignment.

Specific issues:

- The NLP analysis only shows that China-related coverage increased and shifted toward trade topics - this is expected given the objective change in trade relationships.
- No evidence that policymakers actually responded to media coverage rather than to underlying economic incentives.
- No comparison with other countries that experienced similar trade increases without rank changes.
- The mechanism remains a "black box" between media coverage and UN voting.

Required revision: Provide stronger evidence for the salience mechanism through:

- Content analysis of elite speeches/statements referencing media coverage.
- Comparison of media coverage patterns across countries with similar trade growth but no rank changes.
- More direct evidence that decision-makers were influenced by media framing rather than economic fundamentals.

#### 3. Temporal Inconsistencies Requiring Theoretical Development

Critical observation: The data shows Brazil and China converging ideologically from 2001-2015, but then diverging significantly after 2015, despite China remaining Brazil's top trading partner. This pattern seriously undermines the proposed mechanism.

Questions requiring answers:

- If rank salience drives alignment, why does convergence reverse after 2015?
- What explains the divergence during Bolsonaro's presidency when economic ties remained strong?
- How does the theory account for changing political leadership and ideology?

Required revision: Develop theoretical framework addressing temporal variation and scope conditions for when the proposed mechanism operates.

### Minor Issues

#### Data Presentation

- Figure 2: Should use ideal point distance (like Figure 3) to better illustrate Brazil-China convergence.
- The current visualization obscures the key relationship the paper aims to demonstrate.

#### Robustness Concerns

- The placebo tests show no significant effects for 2003, 2005, and 2012, which strengthens the rank effect argument.
- However, more discussion needed of why 2009 specifically represents a meaningful threshold rather than continuous trade growth effects.

### Methodological Strengths to Preserve

The paper's strengths should be maintained:

- Strong identification strategy: The SDiD approach convincingly addresses endogeneity concerns.
- Clear empirical findings: The 42% reduction in ideological distance is substantively important.
- Novel research question: Studying rank effects vs. continuous trade effects is theoretically interesting.

### Recommendation

Major Revisions Required. The paper needs substantial theoretical development to convincingly link media salience to foreign policy outcomes. The empirical strategy for the trade effect is sound, but the mechanism analysis requires significant strengthening. The temporal inconsistencies in the data must be addressed through more sophisticated theorizing about scope conditions and alternative explanations.

The core insight about rank effects in trade relationships has merit, but the paper needs to either:

- Provide much stronger evidence for the media salience mechanism, or
- Acknowledge that rank changes may operate through other channels (economic dependence, elite signaling, etc.) and theorize these alternative pathways.

## Reviewer 2

### Comments to the Author

The manuscript asks how status gains--rather than status deficits--affect foreign policy alignment. It argues that when symbolic milestones, such as China surpassing the United States as Brazil's top trade partner in 2009, are made salient (particularly through media coverage), they generate recognition effects that can shift a state's foreign policy behavior. To test this claim, the author employs a Synthetic Difference-in-Differences design to compare Brazil's UN General Assembly voting before and after 2009 against a counterfactual donor pool, complemented by NLP-based media analysis to trace the mechanism of salience. While this manuscript could potentially make an original contribution to the literature on international status and foreign policy, several areas require refinement.

### Research question

The manuscript asks how upward shifts in international status affect the foreign policy alignment of other states. The author justifies the relevance of this question by noting that most studies of status emphasize anxiety, dissatisfaction, or deficits, whereas this paper shifts attention to the consequences of status gains, a less explored aspect. However, a couple areas remain for improvement:

1. The research question could be framed more tightly. As written, it oscillates between explaining why status gains matter and testing how they affect policy. Clarifying whether the goal is theory-building (a new explanation for why status gains should matter) or theory-testing (a demonstration that symbolic milestones produce measurable effects) would help anchor the paper.
2. The introduction could situate the question more explicitly in broader debates on hierarchy and recognition. At present, the relevance is mostly justified with reference to trade and Brazil; spelling out the implications for IR theory (e.g., what we learn about the politics of recognition or about the symbolic dimensions of global order) would broaden the paper's appeal.
3. The author might also consider positioning the study more clearly in relation to other cases of symbolic thresholds (e.g., mega-events, summitry, "firsts" in diplomacy). Doing so would clarify whether Brazil's experience is idiosyncratic or whether the argument speaks to a more general class of phenomena.

### Engagement with existing research

The manuscript positions itself at the intersection of work on trade and foreign policy. It draws on psychology and behavioral economics to argue that salience and rank effects make symbolic milestones politically meaningful. However, most of the literature review focuses on methodological questions that should not take precedence over theoretical ones. For example:

1. Engagement with the status literature could be more systematic. Important works such as Larson et al (2014), Renshon (2017) and Duque (2018) provide relevant insights into recognition, deference, and the consequences of status-seeking. These studies frame the theoretical terrain and would help readers see where the contribution lies.
2. The argument would also benefit from stronger connections to research on symbolic thresholds and prestige events. There is a rich body of work showing how symbolic moments--hosting the Olympics, achieving nuclear capability, or securing a UN Security Council seat--can amplify recognition or dissatisfaction. Linking to this literature would help clarify whether the "rank effect" identified here is analogous or distinct.
3. The discussion of salience could be bolstered by work on media, agenda-setting, and foreign policy. The empirical mechanism hinges on how trade milestones become salient through media amplification, yet the literature on media effects in IR is only briefly acknowledged. Greater engagement here would make the mechanism more persuasive.
4. While the paper distinguishes itself from coercion- and interest-based explanations, it could do more to clarify complementarities. For example, symbolic thresholds may amplify underlying interest-based dynamics rather than substitute for them. Positioning the argument in this way would make the contribution sharper and less vulnerable to strawman critiques.
5. Use professional language when discussing previous work. The assertion "The first model is just wrong from a causal point of view" when discussing Flores-Macias and Kreps (2013) on p. 5 requires reformulation.

### Argument and theoretical contribution

The central claim is that status gains, once made salient, can reshape foreign policy alignment. The author argues that discrete thresholds are more politically consequential than gradual change, and that media salience plays a key role in translating rank effects into policy shifts. Some areas remain for improvement:

1. The manuscript could more clearly distinguish between rank and status. At times the two terms are used interchangeably, but in IR they imply different processes. Rank change (China surpassing the US in trade) does not automatically produce recognition; it may only create the appearance of recognition. Clarifying this distinction would help situate the argument more firmly in the literature on recognition and hierarchy.
2. The causal mechanism could be unpacked further. Does media salience shape elite beliefs, public opinion, or interest-group lobbying--and which of these channels is most important for foreign policy alignment? The current discussion leaves the audience unclear.
3. The argument sometimes frames symbolic milestones as alternatives to coercion or interest-based explanations. A stronger formulation would present them as complementary mechanisms that help explain why alignment occurs more quickly, or in more domains, than interests alone would predict. This would both sharpen the contribution and mitigate the risk of overstating the role of salience.
4. The argument could do more to spell out its scope conditions. Under what circumstances should we expect symbolic thresholds to matter? Are they most consequential for middle powers like Brazil? For dyads where economic ties are already deep? Being explicit would help readers assess generalizability.

### Empirical analysis

While the empirical strategy is innovative, important questions remain:

1. More work is needed to show that key assumptions behind the empirical strategy are satisfied in this case. This requires diagnostic tests beyond mere eyeballing.
2. More discussion is needed about the persistence of the expected effects of status gains on voting similarity. Figure 3 indicates that such effects are short lived: while ideal point distance reached its nadir around 2013, it has reverted to previous levels since then. Do foreign policy effects subside after the rank effect is no longer salient? What does that say about the argument and its importance?
3. The same figure raises important questions about alternative explanations that merit discussion. The trends in voting behavior coincide with changes in leadership in Brazil. Does alignment with China simply increase under leftist governments but decrease under right-leaning ones? This story seems more consistent with the Fig. 3 trends.
4. More transparency is needed when it comes to key decisions behind the empirical analysis. For example, the SDiD section could be more transparent about donor pool construction. Which countries were included, and why? Were any excluded, and on what grounds? A table summarizing the donor pool and weights would add clarity.
5. Similarly, the media analysis would benefit from more detail about coding and validation. How were texts classified as salient or not, and what measures were taken to ensure reliability? Without this, it is difficult to assess how well the observed patterns capture the intended construct.
6. The mechanism would be strengthened with triangulation from other sources. Evidence from elite statements, legislative debates, or business group lobbying could illustrate how symbolic thresholds translated into policy preferences. Even anecdotal evidence would go a long way to reinforce the plausibility of the causal chain.
7. While the focus on Brazil is justified, the paper could discuss whether similar symbolic shocks have produced effects elsewhere. Even a brief comparative note (e.g., about Africa's trade ties with China, or Japan's symbolic milestones vis-a-vis the US) would help situate the Brazil case in a broader universe.

### Conclusion and contribution

While the paper concludes that symbolic milestones in trade relationships can reshape foreign policy alignment, more work is needed to clarify how the author conducts the empirical analysis and draws inferences from it. Other areas for improvement:

1. The conclusion could more explicitly reiterate the general theoretical payoff: the study shows not only that symbolic thresholds matter, but also that they matter in ways existing status theories have overlooked.
2. Clarify the contribution by distinguishing between salience as recognition and salience as perception. If rank effects only appear to confer recognition, that still has political consequences--but of a different kind than recognition itself.
3. Reflect briefly on the limits of generalizability. Are these dynamics most likely to matter in cases of regional powers, middle powers, or countries with diversified foreign policies?
4. End on a slightly more forward-looking note, highlighting how the study sets the stage for further research on the downstream effects of status gains.
